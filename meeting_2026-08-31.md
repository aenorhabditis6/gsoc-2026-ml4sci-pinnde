## 1. Standard statistical tests

The CaloChallenge metrics were invented for this problem, and their value under
the null is not known in advance — separation power does not read zero when two
samples match, so every number has to be compared against a floor you measure
yourself. The classical tests do not have that problem: their null distributions
are known, so the p-value can be read directly.

Three tests, applied per observable. All three compare the gap between the two
samples' CDFs and differ only in how they reduce that gap to one number:

| Test | What it uses |
|---|---|
| **KS** | the largest gap at any single point |
| **CvM** | the squared gap integrated over the whole curve |
| **AD** | the same integral, weighted towards the tails |

KS was the one you named. The other two had to justify themselves. Detection
rate on a controlled shift, 20 repeats (as presented — see correction above):

```
     N |      ks |     cvm |      ad
   250 |    0.05 |    0.10 |    0.60
   500 |    0.20 |    0.25 |    0.55
  1000 |    0.45 |    0.60 |    0.90
  2000 |    0.70 |    0.75 |    1.00
```

**Anderson–Darling is much the most powerful** — at N=250 it detects 60% of the
time while KS is still at chance. That is not it over-rejecting: its
false-positive rate on an exact null is 0.06, the same as KS. One limitation,
scipy clips its p-value to [0.001, 0.25], so it can say "significant" but not
quote a precise number.

Epps–Singleton was tried and dropped: correct rate, but no power the others did
not already have.

### Checking the assumptions hold on our data

That p-values are uniform under the null is a textbook result, not something
being tested here. What is worth checking is whether the assumptions hold for
our data. The one that genuinely could fail: KS assumes a continuous
distribution, and `sparsity` is a voxel count (`1 − k/6480`), so it has ties.

**How the number is produced:**

```
1. Load all 100,000 showers from each ds2 file and pool them: 200,000.
2. Cut the pool into DISJOINT 1000 + 1000 splits -> 100 of them.
   Disjoint matters: if splits share showers the p-values become
   correlated and the check stops working.
3. Run each test on each of the 7 observables.
4. Per observable, count what fraction of its 100 p-values fall below 0.05.
5. That fraction is the false-positive rate. It should be near 0.05.
```

With 100 splits the standard error on each is 0.022, so scatter from 0.02 to
0.09 is what correct calibration looks like.

### Combining the 7 p-values

With 7 observables you get 7 p-values. Taking the smallest and calling it
significant would be wrong about 30% of the time even when nothing is broken.

We use **Bonferroni** (smallest × 7), which is valid however correlated the
observables are. Fisher's method is more powerful but assumes independence. 

```
correlation between the observables (pooled)
  strongest pair : E_tot / sparsity  = -0.928
```

## 2. Sinkhorn divergence

Unbinned transport distance. Implemented as the debiased
Sinkhorn divergence — entropically regularised optimal transport in the full
7-dimensional space, rather than on 1-D projections like the sliced Wasserstein
distance.

I subtract the self-terms, `S(x,y) = OT(x,y) − ½OT(x,x) − ½OT(y,y)`, because
the raw entropic cost is not zero for identical samples. 

On the model: **0.3819**, against a measured Geant4-vs-Geant4 floor of
**0.4151 ± 0.077**. Within the floor, so it agrees with the other metrics.

### The uncertainty bands, as asked

The bands in the figure at the top are **±1 standard deviation of the observable
across showers within each energy bin** — the physical shower-to-shower spread,
not an uncertainty on the mean. With ~800 showers per bin the uncertainty on the
mean is about **28× smaller**, so the figure understates the agreement.
