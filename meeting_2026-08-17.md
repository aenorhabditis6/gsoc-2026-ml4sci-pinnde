## 1. The data

**CaloChallenge 2022, Dataset 2 — electrons.** <https://zenodo.org/records/6366271>

An incident electron enters a tungsten/silicon sandwich and cascades into an
electromagnetic shower. The readout is 45 layers deep; each layer is a polar grid
of 16 angular wedges × 9 radial rings.

- 45 × 16 × 9 = **6480 voxels** per shower, energy per cell in **MeV**
- Two files of **100,000 showers**, ~1.36 GB each, float64
- `dataset_2_1` for training, `dataset_2_2` held out for evaluation
- `E_inc` spans **1.0 – 992 GeV**, log-uniform (equal statistics per decade)


**Two properties measured in the data itself:**

- **76.33% of voxels are exactly zero**, and *exactly* that same fraction lies
  below 15.15 keV, so the readout threshold is already applied to the
  reference data. 
- The longitudinal profile **peaks at layer 11**, rising then falling: the
  cascade building and dying out, as expected.

The files are gitignored.

## 2. Observables

The CaloChallenge paper (arXiv:2410.21611) lists seven high-level features, but
only five of them are per-shower scalars. The core feature vector is those five
plus two completions:

| Observable | Source |
|---|---|
| `E_tot` — total deposited energy | paper |
| `f_samp = E_tot / E_inc` — sampling fraction | paper |
| `⟨z⟩` — longitudinal centre of gravity (shower depth) | paper |
| `σ_r` — transverse energy-weighted RMS (shower width) | paper |
| `sparsity` — fraction of voxels with zero energy | paper |
| `⟨r⟩` — transverse centre of gravity | in the official code (`GetECandWidthR` returns centre *and* width); the paper's summary lists only the width, where energy sits radially |
| `σ_z` — longitudinal width | addition, derived from the same per-layer energies. ⟨z⟩ alone says where the shower sits in depth but not how spread out it is, and a generator can match the mean while missing the spread. |

The paper's remaining two features cannot be columns of an `(N, d)` matrix, so
they are separate functions: **per-layer energy** `E_ℓ` (45 numbers per shower)
lives in `per_layer_observables`, and the **voxel energy spectrum** (a
distribution over voxels, not a per-shower value) in `voxel_energy_spectrum`.

`per_layer_observables` gives four quantities for each of the 45 layers — energy,
sparsity, radial centre, radial width, 180 columns — reproducing the official
`HighLevelFeatures` definitions (`E_layers`, `sparsity`, `EC_r`, `width_r`) with
the same energy-weighted RMS, so results are comparable with published numbers.

## 3. Finding: the voxel ordering is not what the dataset description implies

![Left: mean energy along the size-9 axis under both candidate reshapes, log scale. reshape(45,9,16) gives a flat sawtooth; reshape(45,16,9) gives a monotonic 50x falloff. Right: 2D map of mean energy over angular and radial bins under the correct reshape, showing uniform vertical bands in angle and a steep gradient in radius.](figures/fig_voxel_order.png)

We know the geometry is 45 layers × 16 angular × 9 radial. What the flat 6480
array does not tell you is the order of the last two axes. Two candidate index
formulas, both legal reshapes of 6480 numbers:

```
Candidate A:  index = layer*(9*16) + r*16 + alpha      →  reshape(45, 9, 16)
Candidate B:  index = layer*(16*9) + alpha*9 + r       →  reshape(45, 16, 9)
```

**B is correct: radius varies fastest, angle is the middle axis.** The dataset
description reads *"9 radial and 16 angular"*, which invites A.

The two axes must look completely different, which makes the test decisive:
radius has to fall off steeply and monotonically (energy concentrates near the
shower axis, set by the Molière radius), and angle has to be flat (azimuthal
symmetry — no preferred direction around the axis). Measured over 2000 showers:

```
Candidate A   size-9 axis = [1.00 0.99 0.97 0.89 0.70 1.00 0.98 0.95 0.63]
                            a sawtooth — nothing physical does this

Candidate B   size-9 axis = [1.00 0.28 0.13 0.08 0.06 0.04 0.03 0.03 0.02]
                            monotonic ~50× falloff — this is the radius
              size-16 axis flat to 0.3% — azimuthal symmetry, as required
```

**Why it matters.** Getting this backwards leaves `⟨z⟩` looking entirely
plausible while silently corrupting **every `σ_r`**. It survives casual review.

**The test that catches it:** a synthetic shower spread across two radii must
give `r_width > 0`, while one spread across two angles must give exactly 0. Under
the transposed order those two cases swap.

### How strongly each piece of evidence supports this

- **Proof — our own measurement.** The radial/angular profile test above, on ds2
  directly. This is the only item that shows the convention is *physically
  correct* rather than merely conventional.
- **Strong confirmation — the official code.** `HighLevelFeatures.py` line 128
  unpacks one layer as `data.reshape(int(self.num_alpha[layer_nr]), -1)`, so
  alpha is slower and r fastest. Lines 49–51 build the radial coordinate as
  `num_alpha * [list of r centres]` — Python list repetition, so r cycles fastest
  across a layer. Two independent places, consistent.
- **Confirms we agree on convention — Sijil's `Dataset2.pt`.** Reshaping the
  Zenodo flat array as `(45, 16, 9)` reproduces his stored 4-D array element-wise
  to float32 precision. If he made the same reshape choice, this shows we
  *agree*, not that we are *right* — valuable for integration, not physical
  evidence.
- **Weakest — a source comment.** `train_calsim_2.py` line 80 annotates
  `showers` as `(N, num_z, num_alpha, num_r)`. It is a comment rather than
  executable code, and it sits on the **dataset_1 photons** loading path; the
  script references no ds2 file at all.

## 4. Finding: a bug in metrics

Shower observables carry **different units**: `E_tot` is tens of thousands of
MeV, `sparsity` is in [0, 1]. SWD and Wasserstein-1 are **scale-dependent**. so SWD, W1 cannot properly measure distance.

## 5. The null floor: what "perfect" looks like on real showers

`dataset_2_1` and `dataset_2_2` are two independent sets of *real* Geant4
showers. Neither is generated. 

N=8000, 7 observables, standardized:

```
mmd  -2.348e-05        auc   0.4971 ± 0.01005
swd   0.02221          chi2  1.096
w1    0.02213          sep   0.0030
fpd   2.0e-04 ± 1.1e-04     kpd   6.4e-06 ± 6.0e-05
```

> Judge any results against this row, not against zero.

This is the midterm stability study applied in practice: a metric value is
meaningless in isolation. SWD = 0.03 looks poor against zero and is essentially
perfect against a floor of 0.022.

## 6. Finding: the separation power has a floor of `n_bins / (2N)`

![Left: separation power against sample size on log-log axes, measured Geant4-vs-Geant4 points falling in a straight line parallel to the n_bins over 2N prediction, from 0.044 at N=500 down to 0.0017 at N=16000. Right: separation power against number of histogram bins at fixed N=4000, rising roughly linearly from 0.0029 at 25 bins to 0.0185 at 200 bins, tracking the same prediction.](figures/fig_sep_floor.png)

left: even if you compare Geant4 against Geant4, separation power is not zero. blue: measured Geant4-vs-Geant4 separation power as sample size N changes; red dashed: predicted 50 / (2N) floor

right:
blue dots: measured floor as number of histogram bins changes
red dashed: predicted n_bins / (2 * 4000)
both go up as bin count increases


`separation_power` is the CaloChallenge's own per-observable statistic,
`S = ½ Σ (p−q)²/(p+q)` on normalized histograms — bounded in [0, 1], 0 = identical
shapes. It measures whether the generated distribution has the same 1D shape as Geant4. It also has a finite-N floor, and a sharp one:

```
    N      measured    n_bins/(2N)    ratio
  500      0.04401       0.05000       0.88
 1000      0.02115       0.02500       0.85
 2000      0.01057       0.01250       0.85
 4000      0.00565       0.00625       0.90
 8000      0.00319       0.00313       1.02
16000      0.00171       0.00156       1.09
```

**The floor falls as 1/N — not the 1/√N that SWD and W1 follow** — and is linear
in the binning: at N=4000, S = 0.00286 / 0.00508 / 0.00996 / 0.01851 for
25 / 50 / 100 / 200 bins.

The law is derivable. For two histograms drawn from the same distribution,
`E[(p̂−q̂)²] ≈ 2p/N` over a denominator of `≈ 2p`, so each occupied bin
contributes `≈ 1/(2N)`:

```
S_floor  ≈  n_occupied_bins / (2N)
```

Measured ratios sit at 0.85–1.09, drifting below 1 for fine binning because empty
tail bins contribute nothing — the *occupied* count is what matters.

**Why this is worth raising.** The CaloChallenge quotes separation powers as bare
numbers. **At N=500 with 50 bins a perfect generator scores 0.044** — larger than
many published per-observable values. Two submissions evaluated at different
sample sizes or binnings are not directly comparable, and a small S is not
evidence of a good model unless it is below `n_bins/(2N)`.

Conclusion: 
A separation power number by itself is not meaningful. We must compare it to the finite-sample floor for the same sample size and same binning.

For example:
with N = 500, 50 bins, even a perfect generator scores about 0.044
with N = 8000, 50 bins, the floor is about 0.003

## 7. The conditional generator on real Geant4 showers

![Two rows of six panels. Top row: conditional mean and spread of each shower observable against normalized log incident energy, Geant4 in blue and flow matching in orange, tracking each other closely across the full energy range. Bottom row: marginal distributions of the same observables, the two histograms overlapping.](figures/fm_calo.png)

`flow_matching/demo_calo.py` learns **p(7 shower observables | E_inc)** from real
ds2 — trained on `dataset_2_1`, scored against held-out `dataset_2_2`, ~20 min on
CPU. `E_tot` spans three decades so it is modelled in log space; everything is
then z-scored with training statistics and inverted after sampling. The condition
is normalized log incident energy in [0, 1].

**Result — at the null floor in every energy bin:**

```
                model      floor

chi2_mean       0.9489     1.096

w1_mean         0.0158     0.0221

energy bin      auc
E 0–25%         0.509 ± 0.020
E 25–50%        0.510 ± 0.011
E 50–75%        0.487 ± 0.020
E 75–100%       0.496 ± 0.009
```

Every quartile is within about one sigma of 0.5 — no slice is separable.
