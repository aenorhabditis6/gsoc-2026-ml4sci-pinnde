## Where this sits, for anyone joining

The flow-matching track of the project. We train a conditional generative model
on CaloChallenge dataset 2 (calorimeter showers, 6480 voxels each, conditioned
on the incident particle energy) and score it in the challenge's own 362
high-level features, against a floor measured by comparing two independent sets
of real Geant4 showers. 1× that floor means as close to Geant4 as Geant4 is to
itself.

**Last Friday, in three lines.** The GPU machine came online, so a full run
takes about 3 minutes and variants can be measured rather than argued about. An
empty calorimeter layer is recorded as an exact value (log10 E = −8), that is
17.8% of all layer-shower pairs, and a continuous model produced one 0.0% of the
time; giving the point mass a band to land in and snapping it back took chi²
from 50.7 to 11.7 times the floor over five seeds. The classifier did not move
(0.909 → 0.894), which is the open problem.

This document covers everything else from the same stretch: the five other
parameterizations we tried, a check that found three defects no distribution
metric could see, and the first attempt at generating raw showers instead of
their summaries.

## 1. Six ways of writing down the same shower

The data never changes. What changes is how a shower is *written* before the
model sees it — absolute energies or fractions of the incident energy, a log
scale or a square root. The model learns whatever we hand it, so this choice
decides what it can express.

![Six parameterizations](figures/week2_parameterisations.png)

*Figure 1. Each bar is one way of writing the same 100,000 training showers.
Height is chi² over the 362 official features, divided by the Geant4-vs-Geant4
floor, so the dotted line at 1× is agreement as good as real data — lower is
better. Green is what we kept, orange what we dropped. Note that green does not
mean "scored best": E_tot-relative is kept for a reason chi² cannot see, and
sqrt widths scored well but became redundant. Whiskers span two runs differing
only in seed.*

| written as | chi² | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| absolute energies | 49× | the baseline |
| **E_tot relative to E_inc** | 49× | **kept** — no chi² gain, but it fixes the energy response |
| every energy relative to E_inc | 59× | worse than doing nothing |
| widths as √x | 25× | helped a lot on its own, then became redundant |
| layer energies as √(E/E_inc) | 31× | the wrong turn |
| **the empty-layer fix** | **10×** | **kept** |

**E_tot relative to E_inc is kept for something chi² does not report.** It
models total energy as a fraction of the incident energy instead of an absolute
number. Pooled chi² does not move. The sampling fraction, how much of the
incident energy the calorimeter records, goes from detectably wrong to
indistinguishable from Geant4: that family scores 0.502 against a measured floor
of 0.488, having been 0.697. The sampling fraction matters for calorimeter
physics, so we take it even though the summary number is flat. It is also an
example of a pooled metric hiding something.

**Every energy relative to E_inc is worse than the baseline** (59× against 49×).
Dividing each of the 45 layer energies by the incident energy sounds like the
same idea applied more thoroughly. It is not: the layer-energy family goes to
0.961, because the interesting variation in a layer's energy is not proportional
to E_inc.

**√ widths scored well and we still dropped it.** 49× → 25×, and it removed the
30% of generated widths that were negative. But 23% of all width values are the
empty-layer 0, so what it mostly fixed was the point mass. Once empty layers are
handled directly, adding it back costs accuracy: 9.4× → 12.6×. Two fixes for the
same problem do not add up, and the better one makes the other harmful.

**The wrong turn: layer energies as √(E/E_inc).** The reasoning sounded right —
energies are non-negative with a spike at zero, the model was generating
impossible negative energies, and squaring on the way out cannot go negative. It
worked exactly as designed: every impossible energy gone. It scored 31×, and its
classifier AUC was 0.992, nearly perfect separation.

The mechanism: in √-space a real lit layer sits around 0.015 and the model
produced 0.00003, a miss of 0.015. The evaluation reads log₁₀ of the energy, and
0.00003 squared is −0.4 in log units where the truth is −8. A small error in the
space we modelled became a large one in the space we are scored in.

The rule to take from it: match the scale the evaluation uses. Optimising a
representation for a property we care about, without checking what the metric
reads, can lose more than it gains.

## 2. A different kind of check: could this shower exist?

Every metric in the project compares *distributions* — chi², separation power,
the classifier, all of them take two piles of showers and ask whether the piles
look alike. None of them ever looks at a single shower and asks whether it is
physically possible.

`pinnde_eval/validate_physical.py` asks exactly that, one shower at a time:

    python -m pinnde_eval.validate_physical runs/atom.npz

Real Geant4 showers run through the identical code as a control. They score
0.00% on every check — which is the point: a check that real data fails is a
broken check, not a discovery.

![Physical validity](figures/week2_physical.png)

*Figure 2. One group per physical rule. Bar height is the percentage of 8,000
generated showers breaking that rule, so lower is better and 0% is correct.
Orange is the baseline, light blue the empty-layer fix, green that plus
computing E_tot from the layers. Real Geant4 has no bars because it breaks none
of these. Bars too small to see have their value printed above them — 0.03% is
small, but it is not zero.*

**The first finding: total energy.** Every generated shower had a total energy
that is not the sum of its 45 layer energies. In real showers the relation holds
to 1 part in 10¹⁵; it is not an approximation, it is the definition of E_tot.
Nothing in the model tied them together, and no distribution metric noticed,
because each of the 46 numbers looked reasonable on its own.

`--derive-total` fixes it by construction: stop modelling total energy, add up
the 45 generated layer energies. 100% → 0%. It does **not** improve any
distribution metric. Its two seeds sit at chi² 9.9 and 14.5 — which, now that we
have measured the spread properly (±20% over five seeds), is ordinary seed
scatter rather than the instability it looked like. We keep it because it is
correct, not because it scores better.

**The second came from a question rather than a run.** Asked how we decide a
layer is empty when eight numbers describe it, the answer is that the energy
decides. That exposed a gap: the rule only ran one way. A layer the energy
called *lit* could still come out with sparsity 1, meaning every one of its 144
voxels is dark — energy sitting in no voxel at all. **63% of baseline showers
had at least one such layer**, and the empty-layer fix barely touched it (58.6%),
because it was never looking in that direction.

The rule is now symmetric: the energy decides both ways, and sparsity 1 is
reserved for layers that really are empty. Measured over five fresh runs, it
fixes more than it was aimed at:

| check | before | with the symmetric rule |
|---|---|---|
| lit layer has a lit voxel | 58.6% | **5.9%** |
| sparsity outside [0, 1] | 44.4% | **7.0%** |

The second row was not the target. Sparsity above 1 means a negative number of
lit voxels, and it had been on the list as a separate problem needing its own
bounded representation — but most of it was the same defect seen from another
angle, and capping a lit layer's sparsity at "one lit voxel" removed six sevenths
of it for free.

## 3. What this says about our metrics

Three kinds of question, and they do not substitute for each other:

| | asks | blind to |
|---|---|---|
| chi², separation power | is each feature's distribution right? | how features relate within one shower |
| classifier AUC | can a classifier tell a whole shower apart? | nothing — but it only gives one number, and does not say what is wrong |
| physical checks | could this shower exist? | whether the distribution is right at all |

The energy-in-zero-voxels defect shows what chi² cannot see. The energy
feature's distribution is fine on its own, and so is sparsity's. chi² inspects
them one at a time and reports nothing. A classifier sees both numbers of the
same layer together and finds the impossibility immediately.

It is tempting to conclude that this is why chi² improved fourfold while the
classifier did not move. We tested that and it is not the explanation. A sample
carrying our marginals with Geant4's exact correlations is still detectable at
0.916, against 0.929 for the model, so the marginals account for nearly all of
it. The correlation defects are real but they are not the binding constraint.

The value of the physical checks is that they name a specific defect, where the
classifier gives one number that only says "still distinguishable".

## 4. Generating the shower itself: first attempt

From the last meeting: an analysis never uses a shower directly, it uses
quantities derived from one, so the reason to generate showers accurately is
that a generator which gets the voxels right is then known to be right for
everything downstream. `flow_matching/demo_voxels.py` does that -- it generates
the **6480 voxel energies** and then computes the 362 features from them with
the challenge's own code, so the numbers land in the same space against the same
floors, and their `DrawAverageShower` can draw the result.

**It does not work yet.** The reasons are specific.

```
  run                        auc   chi2/floor   median E_tot/E_inc
  100k showers, 100k steps  1.000       124x            990,000x
  narrower empty band       1.000       136x                184x
  real Geant4                0.50         1x               0.78x
```

Five times the data and five times the training changed nothing (123.9x to
124.4x), so this is not undertraining. Three measured causes:

1. **The informative values sit in the tail.** 75% of voxels are empty, so a
   voxel's average is near "empty" and the real lit energies end up about three
   standard deviations out. That is where a flow is least accurate, and where
   being off by one unit of z means being off by a factor of 300 in energy.
2. **Most of what we ask the network to predict is noise we added.** Spreading
   the empty mass over the full 6-log-unit gap accounts for up to 51% of a
   sparse voxel's variance, against a real signal of about 11%. Narrowing that
   band improves the energy scale by a factor of 5400.
3. **Log space has no ceiling, and that dominates.** 22.4% of generated layers
   hold more energy than any layer in real ds2, and those layers carry
   essentially 100% of the model's total energy. Drop them and the totals land
   within a factor of 7 of reality instead of 184.

The bulk of the distribution is not far off -- the median generated layer holds
445 MeV against Geant4's 354 -- but the model lights 2.5x too many voxels and
the tail is unbounded.

**This also found a hole in our own checks.** All seven physical rules passed on
showers depositing 10^13 times the beam energy, because every rule tested
consistency (is the total the sum of the layers, is an empty layer empty
everywhere) and none tested conservation. That check now exists, it fires on
100% of these showers, and it would have caught the problem immediately.
Generating voxels gives the consistency rules for free and gives conservation
nothing.

The fix that matches the physics is to bound the output by factorizing the way
published models do: generate the layer energies first, then the normalized
pattern inside each layer, which is bounded by construction. The total is then
set deliberately instead of emerging from 6480 unbounded numbers.

## 5. Next

1. **Decide which metric we are optimising.** A rank-Gaussian feature transform
   makes every marginal exactly right by construction. It halves chi² (11.7× →
   5.9×, with the seed spread falling from 2.3 to 0.1) and removes every
   impossible value, the best physical validity of anything we have built. It
   also makes the classifier worse, 0.894 → 0.956, concentrated in sparsity and
   the centres. The obvious explanation, noise from dithering tied values, was
   tested and ruled out at 1-7% of variance; the cause is not identified. What
   is established is that the challenge's per-feature statistic and a
   whole-shower classifier can move in opposite directions, so "the model
   improved" has to say which one it means.
2. **Bound the sparsity** so a negative voxel count is unreachable.
3. **Bound the voxel model's output**, by generating layer energies first and
   then the normalized pattern within each layer. Section 4 shows why: an
   unbounded tail in log space carries essentially all of the model's energy.
4. **Compare against published CaloChallenge submissions** (Zenodo 15962050),
   with the caveat below stated plainly.

## Honest notes

- **We generate the 362 features, not the 6,480 voxels.** Challenge submissions
  generate voxels and compute the features afterwards. Ours is a strictly easier
  problem, and any comparison with published numbers has to say so.
- `--derive-total` is kept for correctness, not for score, and it makes results
  noisier.
- Snapping makes out-of-range values legal by construction, so "% impossible" is
  no longer a quality measure for the columns that are snapped.
- One run at the wider empty band had 1 shower in 8,000 diverge to NaN; the run
  reported it and dropped it before scoring, so that AUC is over 7,999 showers.
- The symmetric-emptiness fix is tested in code but not yet confirmed by a run,
  and Figure 2's blue and green bars therefore still show the old 58.6%.
